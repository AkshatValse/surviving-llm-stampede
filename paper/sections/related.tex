\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Thrashing, caching and metastability.} The thrashing collapse and the working-set remedy are Denning's~\citep{denning1968thrashing,denning1968working}, and Wilkes analysed the dynamics of paging~\citep{wilkes1973dynamics}; the decomposition analysis of thrashing instability, its stochastic cusp~\citep{nelson1987stochastic,yokohira1989catastrophic} and the choice of a multiprogramming level are classical (\S\ref{sec:background}); LRU's failure on loops is classical~\citep{chou1985evaluation,smaragdakis1999eelru}, as is its weakness on weak-locality patterns~\citep{jiang2002lirs}. Metastable failures were named by Bronson et al.~\citep{bronson2021metastable} and studied in production incidents and modelled by Huang et al.~\citep{huang2022metastable}, whose experiments vary a trigger's magnitude or duration against otherwise matched runs and check whether the bad state persists after the trigger is removed (in runs of 500\,s, triggered at 60\,s, in their replicated-database retry experiments, and for an hour after the trigger in their look-aside cache experiments), the template of our paired test. They were analysed with queueing models, simulation and emulation by Isaacs et al., who also compute a basin for retry storms with a continuous-time Markov chain~\citep{isaacs2025analyzing}, and with continuous-time Markov chains by Alvaro et al.~\citep{alvaro2025formal}; predicted with a queueing model for replicated storage by Habibi et al.~\citep{habibi2024msf}; attributed to destabilizing feedback cycles by Farahbakhsh et al.~\citep{farahbakhsh2025modeling,farahbakhsh2026characterizing}; avoided with bounded concurrency and load shedding in a database simulator by Charapko et al.~\citep{charapko2026execution}; and anticipated with learned indicators by Majumdar et al.~\citep{majumdar2026learning}. An AI system's operating loop has been modelled with cusp bistability and hysteretic recovery, and in an instrumented pipeline experiment it collapses just below a spinodal computed from the measured service law and recovers with a backlog-delayed hysteresis~\citep{vanrooyen2026recoverability}; there the feedback is uncertified output re-entering the load rather than cache loss, and load reduction alone need not restore operation, whereas our rescue keeps the offered load. AWS Lambda's container cache can fail to refill once it is empty; Brooker et al.\ also note that a drop in its hit rate raises latency and, by Little's law, the concurrency and working set the cache must hold, relate this to Denning's thrashing, and guard against it with a concurrency limit and cold-start tests at maximum concurrency~\citep{brooker2023ondemand}. Our trap is the case in which the pool is full and rewritten before reuse. Gibbens, Hunt and Kelly's networks with random alternative routing are a classical case in which a state-dependent priority rule, trunk reservation, removes a congested mode and its hysteresis~\citep{gibbens1990bistability}; their modes are both stable, whereas our congested regime is transient, and the closer analogue of our cap is multiprogramming-level load control (\S\ref{sec:background}).

\paragraph{Queueing theory of LLM serving.} Ao et al.\ give fluid-guided scheduling with threshold admission~\citep{ao2025wait} and show that in a decode-only model, which abstracts away prefix reuse, the eviction-free fixed point can be unstable, with rate-limited admission and length mixing as remedies; its benchmark GPU experiments disable prefix caching~\citep{ao2026service}. In its open-arrival simulations, every load tested between the worst-cycle throughput, the analogue of our $\lmiss$, and the eviction-free capacity accumulated backlog. On our engine, backlog accumulated from an empty start only above the from-empty edge; inside our window the good state persisted from empty for the length of each run, and a burst moved the system out of it. Dai et al.\ prove throughput optimality of work-conserving policies without cache state, and give a cyclic-routing example where work-conserving scheduling fails~\citep{dai2025throughput}; Nie et al.\ give stability conditions under KV memory constraints for independent one-shot requests whose KV cache is freed at completion~\citep{nie2026queueing}; Dong and Cao study flow-controlled admission~\citep{dong2026flow}. Dexter et al.\ analyse FCFS and longest-prefix-match scheduling under prefix reuse and latency constraints~\citep{dexter2025prefix}. Yao et al.\ derive a mean-field LRU hit ratio for multi-turn conversations with exogenous gaps between turns, and note that end-to-end latency may change the observed gaps~\citep{yao2026multiturn}; \S\ref{sec:fp-prior} relates our model to theirs and to the Che approximation, whose criterion CacheRoute states for LLM prefix caches and whose single-characteristic-time form it finds misses a 70B engine's hit rate~\citep{cheng2026cacheroute}. In operations research, service systems whose service slows in congestion, or whose customers return, show bistability and are protected by admission control~\citep{chan2014speedup,dong2015slowdowns,ingolfsson2020fluid}; a session that returns after a tool call is such a returning customer, but there the slowdown is a given function of occupancy.

\paragraph{Prefix and KV mechanisms for agents.} PEEK protects prefixes needed by queued requests and adds a cache-aware scheduler~\citep{xie2026peek}; it documents SGLang's 128-request LPM fallback and runs its heavy-load cells at that depth, and its ``scheduling-induced thrashing'' is LPM breaking ties by arrival order. Continuum keeps a program's KV across a tool call for a time-to-live chosen by a cost model, which may be zero, and reports mean tool times of 925\,ms on SWE-Bench (standard deviation 3550\,ms) and 1923\,ms on BFCL~\citep{li2025continuum,patil2025bfcl}. Sutradhara observes, under Poisson load on vLLM, that prefix hit rates collapse despite context reuse, and attributes this to LRU eviction of the contexts of requests waiting on tool calls~\citep{biswas2026sutradhara}. CONCUR controls the number of active agents in closed batch inference with AIMD, halving its window, to exit the thrashing regime, when KV usage exceeds 0.5 and the hit rate is below 0.2, pausing agents at generation and tool boundaries, and reports SGLang's hit rate falling from 77.7\% at batch 32 to 35.4\% at 40~\citep{chen2026concur}; MARS sets an admission window by AIMD from GPU KV pressure and CPU tool pressure, and reports that under heavier load its baselines' SLO-aware goodput collapses to near zero while their token throughput stays high~\citep{wang2026mars}. On vLLM, Yuan et al.\ report the prefix-cache hit rate falling from 98.85\% to 16.78\% as the maximum number of concurrent Terminal-Bench agents goes from 15 to 20~\citep{yuan2026agentic}, and VAMP calls the sharp rise in time to first token once reusable prefixes outgrow the KV pool the prefix-cache cliff, and measures stock vLLM at a 0.19\% prefix hit rate on a recorded SWE-bench agent replay~\citep{kim2026vamp}. ThunderAgent describes agent collapse as working-set thrashing and pauses whole programs when their KV demand, with programs in tool calls counted at a weight that decays with tool time, exceeds the capacity; it reports that baseline systems collapse in throughput as the number of parallel workflows grows, a closed population~\citep{kang2026thunderagent}, and NVIDIA Dynamo's port of its scheduler aims to avoid thrashing when memory cannot hold every agent's working set~\citep{dhanani2026dynamo}. EfficientAgent applies Denning's working-set principle and load control to the host KV tier of a fixed pool of agents; its abstract describes state written to the host tier and evicted before reuse by other agents' traffic during tool waits, and it reports that waiting behind other agents lengthens the gap between an agent's turns~\citep{shao2026efficientagent}. AgentKV, from its abstract, predicts the onset of collapse at high concurrency from available KV capacity over per-instance context demand and prevents it by predictive admission~\citep{anon2026agentkv}. MISA-T, for RL rollouts under a concurrency ceiling, reports vLLM Router's prefix-cache hit rate falling from 92.4\% to 4.5\% at a high ceiling, describes the positive feedback behind it, a loop between eviction, cold prefill, queue length and session overlap, notes that at prefill saturation the request rate is bounded by prefill throughput over the mean uncached tokens per request, and interrupts the loop with an adaptive admission cap for new sessions, derived from observed KV demand and overload pressure and contracted temporarily on a confirmed hit-rate decline~\citep{hong2026misat}; it has no open arrivals, bursts or recovery test. KAIROS manages GPU frequency and admission for agentic serving under open arrivals on vLLM (Poisson in its characterization, Sec.~3.1; a replayed recorded agent workload in its evaluation, Sec.~7) and reports thrashing at a fixed lowered maximum clock (its Sec.~3.4 and Fig.~5: 660 against 1680\,MHz at 0.08 agents/s), when its controller lowers the clock (its Figs.~10--11), and when the arrival rate rises (its App.~A.4: 0.6 against 0.5 jobs/s), and avoids it by bounding the aggregate context of admitted agents (its Eq.~13: at most 0.95 of capacity, readmitting below 0.9)~\citep{yuan2026kairos}. Its design section (Sec.~6.2.4) describes thrashing as a feedback loop in which throughput drops, requests accumulate and resident context grows, and states that consequently even a short overload can push the instance into a persistently inefficient operating region; it gives this as the motivation for its concurrency bound and does not test it in a controlled experiment. In its Figs.~10--11, at 0.05 jobs/s, the run without thrashing avoidance stays in what KAIROS calls a persistent thrashing regime, completing 0.046 jobs/s, while a baseline at the maximum clock carries the same rate. Its SLO-aware boost (its Eq.~12) restores the maximum clock when an agent falls below its throughput target, but no frequency trace is shown, so whether the trigger was removed is not reported; its three configurations share one replayed workload, but there is no burst trigger and so no matched no-burst arm, and no fixed clock; its Fig.~10 gives one value per configuration, with no error bars or stated number of runs; and when bursty arrivals have already broken its bound KAIROS defers new agents and boosts the clock but reports no recovery from an established trap. Many more systems offload, prefetch, retain, schedule or evict agents' KV, or simulate agent serving; Appendix~\ref{app:supp} lists them.

\paragraph{Position.} The loop is published under a concurrency ceiling or for a closed population (MISA-T, CONCUR, Yuan et al., VAMP, ThunderAgent~\citep{hong2026misat,chen2026concur,yuan2026agentic,kim2026vamp,kang2026thunderagent}), at fixed concurrency (AgentKV, from its abstract~\citep{anon2026agentkv}), and under open arrivals, where KAIROS reports it after a lowered clock or a higher arrival rate and asserts, untested, that a short overload can leave an instance persistently inefficient (above)~\citep{yuan2026kairos}. Session-aware admission, pausing at tool boundaries and hit-rate thrashing signals are implemented or proposed~\citep{kang2026thunderagent,dhanani2026dynamo,hong2026misat,chen2026concur,wang2026mars,yuan2026kairos,li2026tao,jiang2026coda}, to prevent the trap or, in closed populations, to leave it by lowering the number of active agents, which lowers the load itself; where these controllers use two thresholds (ThunderAgent's watermarks, Dynamo's pause and resume levels, KAIROS's 0.95 and 0.9), the thresholds form a controller's dead band; and shedding load is the usual response in production~\citep{huang2022metastable}. We claim none of these. We add (i) a controlled, replicated, registered test of KAIROS's open-system assertion at a fixed clock and pool, following the trigger design of Huang et al.~\citep{huang2022metastable}: two persistent states at one capacity and offered load, entered by a transient burst, against matched no-burst and capped arms on common random numbers, on two pools and two engines; (ii) the rescue of an established open-system trap at the same offered load without shedding, and the persistence of the good state after the cap is removed, hysteresis of the system rather than of a controller, which Carleial and Hellman proposed for bistable channels but did not evaluate~\citep{carleial1975bistable}; (iii) a first-principles thrashing curve in which hit-dependent running reservations produce the cliff and the queue sets the reuse gap on the all-miss branch, with the measured sustaining variable in both states at one load, a cost-model-free cycle accounting relation for its trapped value under explicit common-interval assumptions (a time-domain form of EfficientAgent's backlogged-pool cycle argument~\citep{shao2026efficientagent}; CacheRoute states the underlying revisit criterion for prefix caches whose revisit times are set by routing~\citep{cheng2026cacheroute}), a mean-field bound excluding exactly zero stationary hit probability for the exponential-gap closures considered here without running reservations, and out-of-sample predictions frozen before the H200, pool-sweep, Llama-3.1-8B, basin and rescue results were read by the agents that derived it (the first author had seen the 300k H200 closed-curve values for $n\le32$, which include the predicted peak); and (iv) a measured basin boundary. Against the look-aside cases of the metastability literature, where a cache that has lost its contents fails to refill~\citep{bronson2021metastable,huang2022metastable,farahbakhsh2026characterizing}, our pool is full and rewritten before reuse, an instance of what Huang et al.\ call capacity-degradation amplification. Metastability from a unimodal throughput curve, and bounded admission as its cure, are classical (\S\ref{sec:background}). Ao et al.'s decode-only model abstracts away prefix reuse; extending Yao et al.'s model so that the KV held by running requests, and their service time, depend on the hit rate covers our setting, and \S\ref{sec:fp} does this.
