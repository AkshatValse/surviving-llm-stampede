\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Prefix caching turns multi-turn agent serving into a thrashing workload: the cache that makes follow-up turns cheap belongs to sessions idle in tool calls or waiting in the queue, and admission reclaims it. On SGLang with two pool sizes and on vLLM, at rates the engine carried in the good state for the full length of every no-burst run, a burst of 200 sessions moved the engine into an all-miss state that it did not leave within 7500\,s, while a client-side cap at the peak of $\mu(n)$ drained the same burst; two of these tests were pre-registered and the third registered, and all three pass by their registered rule, although on vLLM the frozen decision rule for the mechanism checks is not met. The same cap, engaged inside an established trap, returned the engine to the good state in 9 of 9 registered runs without shedding a session, and with the cap removed, once the client queues had stayed empty for 300\,s, the good state persisted at the same load while the uncapped controls stayed at zero hits: which state the engine is in depends on its history, not only on its load. Bursts of 25 sessions were absorbed and bursts of 100 trapped the engine, so the good state has a finite basin. A first-principles model in which the queue sets the gap between a session's turns once every turn misses, with no parameter fitted to any $\mu(n)$, hit or trap measurement, reproduces the SGLang curves and the trapped states, shows why models with exogenous gaps cannot produce the cliff, and predicted, before the results were read, the rescue, the basin test and an H200's from-empty edge, though not that GPU's softer tail past the peak or 4 of the 9 rescue release times; on the H200 the window shrank to $(1,1.3)$ times its all-miss capacity, as the model's speed law implies in direction. These results are for synthetic sessions with exponential 5\,s tool times and no shared prefixes, and several frozen tests are pending in this version. Operators should measure $\mu(n)$ for the workload they serve, cap admitted sessions at the smallest population within a few percent of its peak, accepting that a burst then waits at the client, and re-measure when the workload, pool, engine or GPU changes; a cap engaged after a collapse still restores the good state, but preventing the collapse costs no more client delay, and much less with the native pool.
